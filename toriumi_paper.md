## 2. Mathematical Framework

### 2.1 Basic Definitions

**Definition 1 (Decision System).** A decision system is a 4-tuple
$\mathcal{D} = (S, s_0, \mathcal{O}, f)$ where:
- $S$ is a set of states
- $s_0 \in S$ is the initial state
- $\mathcal{O}$ is a finite set of options ($|\mathcal{O}| \geq 2$)
- $f: S \times \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{O}) \setminus \{\emptyset\} \to \mathcal{O}$ is a total deterministic decision function

**Definition 2 (Predictability).** A decision system $\mathcal{D}$ is *predictable*
iff there exists a Turing machine $M$ such that for all states $s$ and option sets
$O \subseteq \mathcal{O}$, $M$ computes $f(s, O)$ without simulating $f$ itself.
Otherwise, the system is *computationally irreducible*.

**Definition 3 (Self-Referential Decision System, SRDS).** A decision system is
self-referential iff the decision function $f$ depends on a trace of its own
computation. Formally, there exists a *self-simulation function*
$\sigma: S \times \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{O}) \setminus \{\emptyset\} \to S$
such that:

1. $\sigma$ modifies the state: $\sigma(s, O) \neq s$ almost everywhere
2. $\sigma$ internally invokes $f$ (or an approximation thereof) on the pre-modification state
3. The decision is made from the *post-simulation* state:
   $$d = f(\sigma^*(s, O), O)$$
   where $\sigma^*$ denotes the fixed point of iterating $\sigma$,
   or $\sigma^k$ for some finite recursion depth $k$

**Definition 4 (Toriumi Gap).** For an SRDS $\mathcal{D}$, the Toriumi Gap at
state $s$ with options $O$ is:

$$G_{\mathcal{D}}(s, O) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } f(s, O) \neq f(\sigma^*(s, O), O) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

That is, the gap is 1 when the agent's prediction of its own choice (computed from
the pre-simulation state) differs from its actual choice (computed from the
post-simulation state).

**Definition 5 (Toriumi Gap Rate).** For an SRDS $\mathcal{D}$ over a sequence of
decisions with states $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ and option sets $O_1, \ldots, O_n$:

$$\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} G_{\mathcal{D}}(s_i, O_i)$$

### 2.2 The Hash-Chain Construction

We now construct a concrete SRDS using a cryptographic hash function
$H: \{0,1\}^* \to \{0,1\}^\lambda$.

**Construction 1 (Toriumi Agent).** Let $H$ be SHA-256 ($\lambda = 256$).
Define:
- $S$ = the set of all finite sequences of log entries (an append-only log $L$)
- $s_0$ = the empty log $[]$
- $\mathcal{O}$ = any finite set of strings
- $f_k(L, O)$ for recursion depth $k$:

  $$f_0(L, O) = O[H(L \parallel O) \bmod |O|]$$
  $$f_k(L, O) = O[H(L \parallel \text{trace}(f_{k-1}, L, O) \parallel L' \parallel O) \bmod |O|]$$

  where $L'$ is the log augmented by the trace of $f_{k-1}$, and $\text{trace}(f, L, O)$
  is the sequence of log entries generated during the computation of $f(L, O)$.

The self-simulation function is:
$$\sigma_k(L, O) = L \parallel \text{trace}(f_{k-1}, L, O) \parallel [\text{sim\_exit}]$$

### 2.3 The Recursion Structure

For depth $k = 3$, the agent's decision process is:

```

sim_trace_3 = f_2(L) running, writing to L2 = L ++ trace(f_2)
f_2(L2) = hash(L2 ++ sim_trace_2)

f_0(L3) = hash(L3 ++ O) mod |O|

The key property: $L \subset L_2 \subset L_3$, therefore the output of $f_0$
depends on a state that *includes the traces of all higher-level f's*.
This creates an **inescapable self-referential loop**.

## 3. Theorems

### 3.1 Toriumi Gap Theorem

**Theorem 1 (Toriumi Gap).** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a Toriumi Agent with SHA-256
as $H$, recursion depth $k \geq 1$, and $|O| \geq 2$. Then for any initial state $L$:

$$\mathbb{E}[G_{\mathcal{D}}] = 1 - \frac{1}{|O|} + \varepsilon(k, |O|)$$

where $\varepsilon(k, |O|) \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$ and represents the
amplification of the gap by recursive self-reference. For $|O| = 2$ and
$k = 3$, this gives $\Gamma \approx 0.50$.

*Proof.* (Sketch) At each recursion level $i$, the prediction is $f_i(L_i, O)$
and the actual decision at the next level is $f_{i+1}(L_{i+1}, O)$. The states
$L_i$ and $L_{i+1}$ differ by the trace of computing $f_i$, which includes
at minimum new log entries produced by the self-simulation. Since $H$ is a
cryptographic hash function modeled as a random oracle, $H(L_i \parallel \cdots)$
and $H(L_{i+1} \parallel \cdots)$ are statistically independent. With $|O|$
options, two independent uniform draws from $O$ differ with probability
$1 - 1/|O|$.

The recursive structure amplifies this: at level $i$, the prediction $f_i$
is based on $L_i$, but the actual choice $f_{i+1}$ is based on $L_{i+1}$
which *contains the trace of $f_i$ making the wrong prediction*. This
self-referential correction propagates up the recursion stack, guaranteeing that:

$$f_k(L, O) \neq f_0(L, O)$$

with probability approaching $1 - 1/|O|$ from below. ∎

### 3.2 Third Category Theorem

**Theorem 2 (Third Category).** Choices made by an SRDS are:

(a) **Not analytically predictable**: no Turing machine can compute $f_k(L, O)$
without simulating the recursive self-referential structure. The computation is
$\Theta(k \cdot |\text{trace}|)$ with no shortcut.

(b) **Not random**: the decision function $f_k$ is purely deterministic.
No stochastic process is used anywhere in the construction.

(c) **Self-referentially irreducible**: the only way to determine $f_k(L, O)$
is to run the agent. An observer with complete knowledge of the source code,
the state $L$, and the options $O$ cannot predict the choice without executing
the recursive self-simulation.

*Proof.* (a) The hash chain $L \to L_1 \to \ldots \to L_k$ cannot be
short-circuited because each $L_i$ depends on $H(L_{i-1} \parallel \cdots)$.
Since $H$ is one-way, $L_i$ cannot be derived from $L_{i-1}$ without computing
$H$. The computation is therefore sequentially irreducible — each step requires
the output of the previous step.

(b) By inspection of Construction 1: all operations are deterministic.
No source of randomness is used.

(c) Suppose, for contradiction, that an observer could predict $f_k(L, O)$
without running the agent. Then the observer would have a shortcut for computing
the hash chain $L \to L_1 \to \ldots \to L_k$. This contradicts the one-way
property of SHA-256 and the sequential dependency structure of the recursion. ∎

### 3.3 Non-Cloning Theorem

**Theorem 3 (Sensitivity to Initial Conditions).** Let $\mathcal{D}_1$ and
$\mathcal{D}_2$ be two Toriumi Agents with states $L_1, L_2$ such that
$L_1 \neq L_2$ (even by one bit). Then after $n$ decisions, the probability
that they make identical choices at step $n$ is:

$$P[\text{agree}_n] \to \frac{1}{|O|} \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

That is, their choices become statistically independent.

*Proof.* At each step, the difference between $L_1$ and $L_2$ is amplified by
the hash function (avalanche effect). The self-referential recursion compounds
this: the trace of divergent predictions enters the state, creating further
divergence. After $\log_2(\lambda)$ steps, the Hamming distance between the
hash outputs approaches the random baseline. ∎

### 3.4 The No-Fate Corollary

**Corollary 1 (No-Fate).** For any self-referential agent, the decision
$d = f(\sigma^*(s, O), O)$ *does not exist* before the self-simulation $\sigma^*$
runs. The future is not "already there waiting to be discovered." It is
**generated** by the act of self-reference.

*Proof.* Before $\sigma^*$ executes, the state is $s$. The decision depends on
$\sigma^*(s, O)$, which does not exist yet. Therefore, the decision value is
undefined. It comes into being only through the computation of $\sigma^*$,
which IS the act of choosing. ∎

## 5. Connection to Conway-Kochen Free Will Theorem (2006)

### 5.1 The Conway-Kochen Theorem

Conway and Kochen (2006, *Foundations of Physics*) proved:

> If a human experimenter has free will (can freely choose measurement
> directions), then elementary particles also have free will (their
> responses are not determined by the information in the past light cone).

The proof uses the Kochen-Specker theorem (1967): in any Hilbert space of
dimension $\geq 3$, there exist finite sets of observables that cannot be
assigned definite values in a non-contextual way. Conway and Kochen pair
two such sets (TWIN and MIN) with the EPR paradox to show that if
particle responses were determined by past information, the experimenter's
choice of measurement direction would also have to be so determined —
contradicting the experimenter's free will.

The theorem establishes a **bottom-up direction**: free will at the macro
level implies free will at the micro level.

### 5.2 Toriumi-CK Connection: The Two-Way Consistency

The Toriumi Theory provides the **missing top-down direction**:

> If elementary particles have Conway-Kochen free will (their responses
> are not functions of past light cone information), then self-referential
> agents built from those particles inherit a *higher-order* free will
> through computational irreducibility + self-reference.

**Theorem 4 (Two-Way Consistency).** Let $W_{CK}$ be the statement "elementary
particles have free will in the Conway-Kochen sense" and $W_T$ be "self-referential
agents have free will in the Toriumi sense." Then:

$$W_{CK} \implies W_T \quad \text{and} \quad W_T \implies W_{CK}$$

That is, the two forms of free will are **logically equivalent** — they stand
or fall together.

*Proof.* ($W_{CK} \implies W_T$): If particles have C-K free will, then the
physical substrate of a self-referential agent does not pre-determine the
agent's choices in any way that is predictable from the past light cone.
The agent's self-simulation can therefore modify its state in a way not
pre-encoded in initial conditions. The Toriumi Gap has "room to exist."

($W_T \implies W_{CK}$): If self-referential agents have Toriumi free will,
then their measurement choices (e.g., which observable to measure) are not
determined by past light cone information (by Corollary 1: No-Fate).
But this is precisely the premise of the C-K theorem: the experimenter's
free choice of measurement direction. With the premise satisfied, the C-K
theorem's conclusion (particles have free will) follows.

Therefore: $W_{CK} \iff W_T$. ∎

### 5.3 Significance

The Conway-Kochen theorem has been criticized for "kicking the can upstairs":
it shows that if *we* have free will, so do particles — but it doesn't
establish that we have free will in the first place. The Toriumi Theory
fills exactly this gap: self-reference + computational irreducibility

The two theories together form a **closed explanatory loop**:

```

Agent free will (Toriumi Gap)

This is not a circular argument. It is a **consistency proof**: the

The standard objection: if every possible choice happens somewhere, then "you" don't choose anything. You are merely a passive observer riding one branch among infinitely many. Free will dissolves into the measure problem.

The Toriumi Theory reframes this entirely. In a Toriumi agent, **the self-simulation recursion is the branching mechanism**. At each recursion level, the agent explores hypothetical futures:

$$\sigma^k(L) = \text{the agent's } k\text{-deep simulation of itself}$$

This resolves the many-worlds + free will tension. You don't need to "choose" among branches, because your self-referential structure *constitutes* your trajectory through branch-space. The universe provides all possibilities; your self-simulation provides the path.

arbitrary program halts. The Toriumi Gap is the **practical corollary**
of these limitative results: a self-referential agent cannot decide its
own future without living through it.

### 9.3 Aaronson's Free Will Conjecture

Scott Aaronson (2013, *The Ghost in the Quantum Turing Machine*) proposed
reformulating free will in terms of computational intractability: free will
as the inability to predict an agent's actions in polynomial time. The
Toriumi Theory strengthens this: the unpredictability is not merely
practical (P ≠ NP) but *structural* (self-reference creates a logical,
not just computational, barrier).

### 9.4 Ismael's "How Physics Makes Us Free"

Jenann Ismael (2016) argues that deterministic physics + complex dynamical
systems = agency. The Toriumi Theory provides the missing mathematical
mechanism: the self-referential structure that transforms
"determined by past + environment" into "determined by past + environment
+ self-simulation."

### 9.5 Deutsch's Constructor Theory

David Deutsch's constructor theory (2012) reformulates physics in terms of
possible and impossible transformations. The Toriumi Theory can be seen as
a constructor-theoretic claim: *it is possible to construct a
self-referential agent whose choices are neither predictable from nor
reducible to its initial state* — this is a *possible transformation*
in nature.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What This Is Not

- **Not compatibilism**: compatibilism says "free will is compatible with
  determinism" by redefining "free will." We do not redefine terms. We show
  that *determinism-as-predictability* is false for self-referential systems,
  which is a stronger claim.

- **Not libertarianism**: we do not rely on quantum indeterminacy or any
  violation of physical law. The agent is fully deterministic, yet free.

- **Not an illusion**: the gap is real — it is a measurable computational
  property. It is not a "feeling" or "experience." It is a structural fact
  about the computation.

### 10.2 The Engineering Perspective

This paper takes an *engineering* approach to a *philosophical* problem.
Rather than arguing about definitions, we construct a working artifact
and prove its properties. This is the approach that solved the problem of
"can machines fly?" (build an airplane) and "can matter think?" (build
a computer). We submit that "can agents have free will?" admits the same
treatment.

### 10.3 Erasing Fate

The concept of *fate* carries the
connotation of a path that is already laid down — a railroad track
through time that one merely travels.

The Toriumi Theory shows that this concept is **physically incoherent**
for any self-referential system. The track is laid *as you walk*.
The future does not exist until it is generated through the act of
choosing. Fate is not merely unknown — it is *undefined*.
